Open work

Personal projects built from publicly available data. They are not associated with my work for any current or past employer and do not represent their views.

  • BC's electricity balance: the drought, imports and demand

    Open data analysis in Python, with interactive charts and a monthly dashboard, 2026

    The 2023 drought was a once-in-100-years low on the Fraser. Low inflows explain most of the 11 TWh fall in BC's hydro output. Clustering, a regime model and machine learning show how trade with the US moved hour by hour. Six months out, demand forecasts miss by 3.8% on average, and their ranges hold as often as stated.

  • Household gas demand in Canada since 1981: price, efficiency and forecasts

    Open data analysis in Python, with interactive charts and a monthly dashboard, 2026

    A 10% higher gas price cuts BC households' use by about 1.2% in the first year, and two common traps overstate that. Partial pooling across six provinces, heating-curve models and machine learning show where the long fall in use came from, and a year's demand is forecast with an average error of 5-7%, with ranges from past errors.

    Project pageDashboardCode

  • How much of BC's fine-particle pollution is wildfire smoke?

    Open data analysis in Python, with interactive charts and a seasonal dashboard, 2026

    About a third in 2018 and 2023, by a rule checked against BC's own list and satellite smoke maps. Without smoke, PM2.5 has slowly fallen since 2005; with it, there is no trend.

  • What minimum wage rises did to pay and jobs in Canada since 2006

    Open data analysis in Python, with interactive charts and a monthly dashboard, 2026

    After 22 large rises in nine provinces, low-paid jobs moved up the pay scale and total employment held. A 10% rise moved teen employment by −0.3% (−2.4% to +4.0%).

  • Productivity and pay by sector in Canada

    Open data analysis in Python, 2026

    Whether pay in Canada follows productivity, across 13 business sectors and 10 provinces from 1997 to 2024. Over the whole period, compensation and productivity grew by about the same amount, but a sector's own productivity gains show up mostly in lower relative prices, with little in its workers' pay. Education and minimum wages account for more of the rise in pay since 2006 than sector productivity does.

  • The Indigenous income gap in Canada across four censuses, and what can be said about it

    Open data analysis in Python, 2026

    An extension of a graduate econometrics assignment from the 2006 Census to 2021, on Statistics Canada's public microdata. Among people with the same measured characteristics, the wage gap narrowed from about 25% to about 14% for men and from about 10% to about 4% for women. The results describe a gap and don't identify causes. The people counted as Indigenous also changed across these files, so this is not one group followed over time, and the write-up measures how far that could matter.

  • How three filters read Canada's business cycles, and what they make of 2020

    Open data analysis in Python, with an interactive chart, 2026

    How Canadian output, consumption, investment, government purchases and net exports rise and fall around their long-run growth, from 1961 to 2026, on Statistics Canada data. Investment accounts for most of the swings in output, and net exports lean weakly against them. The 2020 fall looks different under each of three common filters, and one of them shows a boom two years later that comes from the method.

  • Testing the CAPM and the Fama-French factor models through 2026

    Open data analysis in Python, 2026

    A redo of a graduate financial economics exercise on data through September 2026. It reproduces the original result, then tests the CAPM, three- and five-factor models with GRS, Fama-MacBeth and Hansen-Jagannathan distance. All three are rejected on size and value portfolios. The factors fit those portfolios much better than beta does, but on industry portfolios they make the alphas larger.

    Project pageCode

  • Does narrowly winning a federal riding help a party win it again?

    Regression discontinuity replication and extension in Python, 2022 and 2026

    A party that narrowly wins a federal riding is about 12 points more likely to win it next time, across elections from 1872 to 2025. I reproduced my 2022 replication of Kendall and Rekkas exactly, then added data-driven bandwidths, validity checks and five new elections, linking ridings across two redistributions by the voters they share. Since 2011 the data can't separate the estimate from earlier periods, and it depends on how the redistributions are handled.

    Project pageCode

  • Checking my Bitcoin forecasts

    Rebuild of an MA paper in Python, 2022 and 2026

    My 2022 paper reported a regression score of 0.9994 for Bitcoin's closing price. The rebuild shows that score came from using the same day's high and low, and tests the models again using only earlier information. Across 3,582 days to November 2026, none of them beats a forecast of no change.

    Project pageCode

  • The welfare cost of Proof of Work, revisited

    MA thesis re-analysis in Python, 2021 and 2026

    My MA thesis calibrated a model of Proof-of-Work payments and found Ethereum more costly than Bitcoin, 2.17% of consumption against 1.43%. Checking it in 2026, I found errors in the Ethereum inputs that decided that answer, along with a comparison of different years. Corrected and on the same year, 2020, Ethereum costs 0.57% and Bitcoin 0.74%, with most of the cost in both being mining.

Academic work

  • Bitcoin price forecasting using different methods

    MA paper

    Simon Fraser University, 2022. Compared regression, ARIMA and logit forecasts of Bitcoin's price. A 2026 rebuild found that the paper's headline result, a score of 0.9994, came from look-ahead leakage: the regression used the same day's high and low to predict that day's close. Using only earlier information, no model beats a forecast of no change. The rebuild.

  • Welfare analysis of proof-of-work: Ethereum and Bitcoin

    Graduate research

    Allameh Tabataba'i University, 2021. Compared which protocol costs its users less, with blockchain data collected in Python and simulations in MATLAB. The 2026 re-analysis corrects calibration errors in the thesis, mainly in the Ethereum inputs, and puts both coins on 2020 data. Ethereum's welfare cost goes from 2.17% to 0.57% of consumption and Bitcoin's from 1.43% to 0.74%, so Ethereum comes out somewhat lower, where the thesis found Bitcoin lower. The corrected re-analysis.
